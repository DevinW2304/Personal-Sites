Whole Foods Mobile Experience

Mobile Web · UX & Data Modeling

A mobile-first grocery shopping prototype that recreates key parts of the Whole Foods experience: browsing, product details, cart, and a lightweight payment flow, all powered by a custom JSON data layer.

Role: UX / UI, Front-end dev Timeline: ~2–3 weeks (course project) Tech: HTML, CSS, JS, JSON

Problem & Goals

Modern grocery apps pack a lot of information into small screens: categories, promotions, dietary filters, pickup vs. delivery, and personalized accounts. For this project, the goal was to:

  • Design a mobile layout that feels calm instead of cluttered.
  • Let users move smoothly from browsing to cart and payment.
  • Model the data in a way that keeps the UI flexible and testable.

User Research & Testing

Before locking in the flows, I ran a small round of user studies to understand how people actually move through grocery apps and where they get stuck. The focus was on navigation clarity, cart visibility, and how confident people felt about prices and product details.

  • Participants: a small group of classmates and friends who shop for groceries online at least occasionally.
  • Format: short task-based sessions (around 15–20 minutes) with think-aloud feedback.
  • Prototype: an early version of the mobile web flow with categories, product detail, and cart.

Example tasks:

  • “Find a deli item you’d actually buy and add it to your cart.”
  • “Change the quantity of something in your cart without losing your place.”
  • “Check what time slots are available for pickup or delivery.”

What I heard:

  • People wanted a clearer separation between category browsing and search, instead of everything blending together.
  • Users kept looking for a persistent cart summary so they could see totals without committing to checkout.
  • Product cards needed more scannable info (price, size, tags) so people didn’t have to open each detail page to compare.

Changes I made based on testing:

  • Gave the cart a clearer entry point and a more obvious “Review order” step.
  • Adjusted the category layout and labels to make deli/produce/other sections easier to skim.
  • Updated product tiles to surface key info (price, unit, tags) directly in the list view.

Phased Development

I treated the project like a mini product cycle instead of a single sprint. Breaking it into phases made it easier to keep the UX, data model, and visual design aligned.

  1. Phase 1 – Framing & IA
    Defined the core user journey (browse → product → cart → review) and sketched low-fidelity screen flows. I also mapped out the key entities (products, stores, users, slots) that the eventual DataAPI would need to support.
  2. Phase 2 – Clickable Prototype & User Studies
    Built an early HTML/CSS prototype focused on category browsing and product detail pages. Ran short user tests to understand navigation pain points and what information needed to be visible at a glance.
  3. Phase 3 – DataAPI & Flow Integration
    Implemented the JSON-backed DataAPI and wired it into the key screens: shop, category, search, product detail, and cart. This phase turned the prototype into something that behaves like a mini app instead of static mockups.
  4. Phase 4 – Polish & Microcopy
    Refined spacing, hierarchy, and microcopy to keep the experience calm and legible on a small screen. Tuned card layouts, button labels, and error states so the flows would make sense without extra explanation.

Key Flows

  • Onboarding & Access: entry screens that set expectations and route users to login or registration.
  • Browse & Search: category browsing and search pages that pull product data from a shared JSON layer.
  • Product Detail: product pages that surface key info (price, inventory, tags) and let users add to cart.
  • Cart & Payment Preview: a review cart and simple payment preview to simulate checkout.
  • Profile: a lightweight account area for basic user info and mock order history.

Implementation Details

To keep things maintainable, I built a small internal DataAPI that sits on top of a set of JSON files. Instead of hard-coding products into each page, the UI pulls from:

  • products.json – base product catalog with categories, prices, and tags.
  • inventory.json – stock levels per store and product.
  • stores.json – store locations and IDs.
  • delivery_slots.json – pickup/delivery window data.
  • promotions.json – deals that can be applied to products.
  • users.json – simple mock users for login and profiles.

Each page calls DataAPI.loadEverything() once, then uses helper methods like getProductsByCategory(), searchProducts(), and getEffectivePrice() to render the UI. This mirrors a real-world separation between data and presentation, even though everything is static.

What I Learned

  • How quickly grocery UI can become overwhelming if hierarchy and copy aren’t carefully tuned.
  • The value of modeling data first so that UI and flow changes don’t require rewriting everything.
  • How small user tests can surface very practical fixes, like clearer cart affordances and better product tiles.
  • Ways to bring an “app” feel into a pure HTML/CSS/JS prototype using a consistent mobile shell.
